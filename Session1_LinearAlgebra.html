Why This Course Exists

Four foundational concepts. Four fields. One equation.

This course is built on four concepts that are settled science — textbook material in their respective fields, none in dispute by working scientists in those fields.

  • Boltzmann's distribution (1877) — statistical mechanics
  • Maximum entropy principle (Shannon 1948, Jaynes 1957) — information theory
  • Softmax attention mechanism (Vaswani et al., 2017) — machine learning
  • Luce's choice axiom (1959) — choice theory / econometrics

These four pillars are mathematically the same equation — the convergence equation. The course teaches you to see the identity, then shows that the identity, combined with five physical conditions, prescribes a complete neural architecture for predicting molecular binding: the Specificity Foundation Model (SFM).

The Convergence Equation

Four independent derivations. One function.

Four derivations, four fields, 140 years apart. Not analogies — an algebraic identity.

Why No Existing Training Prepares You

A structural gap, not a failure of anyone's preparation
  • Standard ML courses teach attention — but not its identity with Boltzmann
  • Standard biophysics teaches Boltzmann — but not its identity with attention
  • Standard information theory teaches max entropy — but not its prescriptive consequences for neural architectures
  • No existing curriculum stitches these together — because none was designed to. The implications of Level 3 alignment — where the architecture is the governing equation rather than approximating it — have to be learned explicitly.

The math used in this course is at the level of college algebra and basic calculus — dot products, exponential functions, derivatives. Detailed step-by-step derivations are provided in writing for every key result. Anyone in the lab can take this course. Wet-lab researchers, computational biologists, and immunologists will all start from the same place.

Final Project — Build Your Own SFM

With Claude Code · using CALM as the reference implementation

Each of you builds an SFM on your own molecular recognition data — antibody–antigen pairs from repertoire or display screening, TCR–pMHC pairs, peptide–MHC pairs, cytokine or other ligand binding to receptors, whatever fits your project.

  • You decide the domain-expert decisions: encoder selection, data curation, masking strategy, train/test split design and leakage prevention, hard-negative strategy, evaluation design
  • The architecture itself does not change — that part is prescribed by the convergence equation
  • You evaluate with retrieval metrics, out-of-distribution splits at sequence-identity-clustered thresholds, and pseudo-prospective validation against held-out experiments
  • You present your results to the lab. We then discuss the cross-domain comparison: what training behaviors emerge from your data, what they tell us about the framework, and what comes next.

This is the only test of understanding that matters: did you build a model that works on data nobody has seen before?

SFMs as Infrastructure

A method like PCR or CRISPR
Broad applicability
Any molecular recognition system at thermal equilibrium — antibodies, TCRs, MHC, TFs, miRNAs, CRISPR guides, enzymes, drug–target pairs. Six SFM domains demonstrated, four more in active development.
Low resource
CALM trained on ~4,000 antibody–antigen pairs vs. CLIP's ~400,000,000 image-text pairs. Single GPU node, pretrained encoders. Bottleneck is data curation by a domain expert.
Open source
CALM, the SFM template, training and evaluation pipelines, validation harness, pretrained encoders — all public. No proprietary infrastructure needed.
The natural division of labor
AlphaFold and the structure predictors solve structure. ESM-2, AntiBERTy, DNABERT-2, MoLFormer, RNA-FM solve sequence representation. Neither solves specificity — which partner does this molecule recognize from a pool of candidates? SFMs sit on top of this infrastructure and produce the specificity prediction itself.
Ten years from now: every recognition problem in biology has an SFM.
The model you build at the end of this course is the prototype.
Part 2 · The Mathematics

Linear Algebra for Machine Learning and Molecular Recognition

One Equation Governs Both

  • The softmax attention mechanism in transformers (Vaswani et al., 2017)
  • is mathematically identical to
  • the Boltzmann distribution governing molecular binding (Boltzmann, 1877)
  • This identity is not approximate — it is algebraic
  • This course will prove it, derive its consequences, and show it prescribes a complete neural architecture across ten biological domains

Four Operations Build the Entire Course

Everything that follows is constructed from these
  • Dot product: measures compatibility between two vectors
  • Matrix multiplication: computes all pairwise compatibilities simultaneously
  • Cosine similarity: normalizes the comparison to a [−1, +1] scale
  • Softmax: converts compatibility scores into a probability distribution

What Is a Vector?

Just an ordered list of numbers — and a direction in space
  • A vector is an ordered list of numbers. Nothing more mysterious than that.
  • You already use them. Describe an amino acid by its measurable properties:
    • Leucine → [ hydrophobicity 3.8,  volume 167,  charge 0,  polarity 0 ]
    • Lysine  → [ hydrophobicity −3.9, volume 169, charge +1, polarity 1 ]
  • Each number is one coordinate; each property is one axis. The list places the molecule as a point — or an arrow from the origin — in "property space."
  • Two molecules with similar properties → nearby points → arrows that point the same way.
  • The whole game of this course: turn molecules into geometry, then compare them geometrically.

What Is a Compatibility Score?

In every system, it is a dot product of feature vectors
  • Antibody surface features · Antigen surface features  =  binding compatibility
  • TF positional preferences · DNA nucleotide identities  =  binding energy
  • Transformer query vector · key vector  =  attention score
  • The dot product is the universal compatibility measure
  • This is not a modeling choice — it follows from the bilinear decomposition of binding energy (Lecture 5)

The Magnitude Problem

Should longer vectors always mean higher compatibility?
$$\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \|\mathbf{a}\|\,\|\mathbf{b}\|\,\cos\theta$$
  • The dot product conflates alignment ($\cos\theta$) and magnitude ($\|\mathbf{a}\|\,\|\mathbf{b}\|$)
  • A very "loud" vector dominates all dot products regardless of direction
  • In molecular binding: we care about direction (what features match), not magnitude
  • Solution: normalize to unit length → dot product = pure alignment

The Conversion Problem

We have scores. We need probabilities.
  • Agent has compatibility scores with $M$ candidates: $\mathbf{s} = [s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_M]$
  • We need a probability distribution: $\mathbf{P} = [P_1, P_2, \ldots, P_M]$
  • Requirements:
    • All probabilities positive
    • Sum to 1
    • Accept any real-valued scores — including negatives (unrestricted domain)
    • Higher score → higher probability
    • Relative preference between two candidates shouldn't change when a third enters or leaves
  • How many functions satisfy all five?  →  Exactly one (Lecture 6)

The Identity

Boltzmann (1877) = Vaswani (2017)
Molecular binding — Boltzmann, 1877
$$P(\text{bind } j) = \frac{\exp(-\Delta G_j / k_B T)}{\sum_k \exp(-\Delta G_k / k_B T)}$$
Transformer attention — Vaswani et al., 2017
$$A_j = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{k}_j / \tau)}{\sum_k \exp(\mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{k}_k / \tau)}$$
Score identification
$$s_j = -\Delta G_j / k_B T = \mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{k}_j / \tau$$

Same function. Same structure. Same parameter.
Derived independently, 140 years apart.

Why Represent Molecules as Vectors?

From one-hot encoding to distributed representations
  • One-hot encoding: A = [1,0,0,0], C = [0,1,0,0], G = [0,0,1,0], T = [0,0,0,1]
    • Every pair has dot product = 0 — all biological information discarded
  • Distributed representation: each token → dense vector in $d$ dimensions
    • Tokens in similar contexts → similar vectors
    • Geometric relationships capture functional relationships
  • Mikolov et al. (2013): king − man + woman ≈ queen
    • Meaning is geometry

From Words to Molecules

The lineage of representation learning
  • 2013 — Word2Vec (Mikolov et al.): words → vectors, similarity = dot product
  • 2019 — BERT (Devlin et al.): contextual word vectors via masked language modeling
  • 2021 — ESM-2 (Lin et al.): protein sequences → contextual residue vectors
  • 2021 — CLIP (Radford et al.): two encoders (image + text), contrastive learning
  • 2026 — CALM (Lee et al.): two encoders (antibody + antigen), dot product = binding compatibility
  • The mathematical operation at every step: the dot product

What Makes a Good Representation?

Bengio, Courville & Vincent, IEEE TPAMI 2013
  • A good representation disentangles the factors of variation relevant to the task
  • After disentangling: a linear classifier (the dot product) suffices
  • Given bilinearity (Lecture 5): the energy IS a dot product in raw sequence space
    • Example: TF–DNA binding via position weight matrices
  • Emergent bilinearity (Lecture 5): the encoder must learn a representation where energy becomes a dot product
    • Example: antibody–antigen binding via contrastive co-embedding
  • This distinction predicts measurably different scaling behavior
